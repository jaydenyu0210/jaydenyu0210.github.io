PROJECT OVERVIEW

iNurra, Home Care After the Hospital

iNurra is an iOS app that turns one photo into a home care plan. You point the camera at a discharge sheet, a pill bottle, a blood pressure monitor or a rash, and it comes back with plain-language medications, a dosing calendar, readings with their normal ranges, and a photo-to-photo record of whether a wound is healing. It shipped to the App Store.

I designed and built all of it alone, from the first screen to the store submission: twenty screens, eleven database tables, the extraction prompt, the design tokens, the privacy policy and the consent gate. I am not a clinician, so I framed the problem around what a patient is physically holding when they get home rather than around clinical practice. The app never gives a diagnosis. It tells you what a reading means and when to call someone, and that boundary is written into the model's own instructions.

Product Designer, Frontend, Backend

4 Months

Designed and built solo, and AI-paired throughout with Claude Code.

The Design Challenge

Someone is discharged at three in the afternoon holding a folder. Inside it is a page of instructions in type meant for a chart, three prescriptions with directions written as BID and TID, a follow-up appointment, and a line telling them to check their blood pressure every day. They are tired, possibly in pain, and the person who explained all of it is already with another patient.

The premise I designed against, and I want to be clear that it is a premise rather than a study I ran, is that most of what gets recorded as non-compliance is confusion. Someone who cannot tell which of three white pills is the one for blood pressure, or does not know that a reading of 148 is high, is not refusing care. That reframing decided the whole product. The job was not to remind people, it was to translate.

The second constraint came from the answer rather than the question. The only thing that can read a curled pill-bottle label in a second is a language model, and a language model that quietly writes a dosing schedule into someone's calendar is a safety problem rather than a feature. Most of designing this app was deciding in advance where the model is allowed to act on its own and where a person has to sign.

Identifying User Pain Points

I want to be plain about this. There was no research budget and no access to patients, and there are no interview transcripts behind this project. What I had were the artifacts themselves, the discharge sheets and prescription labels and home monitors, and a clear picture of who would be holding the phone over them. Everything below is a premise I committed to and then defended in the interface, not a finding.

01

The patient who just got home

Tired, sore, often reading through the wrong glasses, needing one question answered: what do I take, and when. Home is five cards and nothing else, and the medication list leads with your own photo of the bottle rather than a generic pill icon.

02

The reader who would rather listen

Small type and dense clinical prose are the first barrier, before anything else is. Every content card carries a speaker button, every paragraph the model writes is broken one sentence per line, and you can hold to talk instead of typing.

03

The family member holding the phone

Usually the adult child, tracking a parent's wound photos or a child's eczema. The describe step invites narration in someone else's words, but there is no dependent account in the schema, so caregiving happens inside the patient's own login.

The Decisions That Shaped It

Four choices did most of the work. Each one came out of a pain point above, and each one cost me something I would have liked to keep.

Nothing is saved until the person says yes

01

The model reads the photo and proposes: three medications, one appointment, two readings. None of it touches the database. The user gets a counts screen first, then a card for every single item with a read-aloud button and a detail dialog, and only Confirm all and Save writes a row. It cost me the demo-friendly version where you take a photo and your calendar fills itself in, and someone photographing five bottles now taps through five reviews to get home. I would take the slower flow every time, because a receipt you can check is a completely different object from an assistant that edits your schedule while you are not looking.

The user never says what kind of document this is

02

The upload screen still contains a five-option document-type picker with radio buttons. It is dead code. I built it and then never routed to it, because asking someone discharged an hour ago whether they are holding a discharge summary or doctor's notes is the exact confusion the product exists to remove. So the model classifies instead, and from late January it classifies multi-label rather than single-type, since one discharge sheet honestly contains a prescription, a follow-up task and a document at the same time. What I gave up was the free accuracy of a user-declared type, so I had to defend against the model instead: an enum guard on the way into the database, a valid skeleton record when the JSON fails to parse, and tolerance for the model renaming its own fields between runs.

No tab bar. One camera, one question.

03

There is a route group in this app called tabs and it has never once rendered a tab bar. Five equal tabs is five decisions asked of someone before they have done anything, so navigation became a home screen of five cards plus a fixed bottom bar holding a camera and a pill that says Ask Nurra. The model I wanted in someone's head was show it, or ask it. The cost is discoverability, and I paid it in full: a complete full-screen chat is still sitting in the app with no button leading to it, because the footer replaced the one that did.

Read-aloud is a control, not a setting

04

There is no accessibility screen in iNurra to go and switch something on. A speaker button sits on the medication card, the metric card, the document card, the condition card and inside every review dialog, and each one speaks a script assembled from the structured fields rather than reading the screen. A blood pressure result is spoken as higher than normal, not as two numbers. I used server-side speech instead of the device voice so it sounds the same everywhere, and added one global stop event so audio never talks over navigation. The cost shows up two sections down, where I admit I built an app that reads itself aloud and never labeled its own buttons.

The Camera I Built and Deleted

The hardest screen in the app is the one where you photograph a rash on your own arm and want to know whether it is getting better. I spent a day building the answer I thought that needed, and threw it away the same day.

What Went Wrong — On February 5 I shipped a custom camera for wound and rash photos. It had a viewfinder with a ruler grid whose scale tracked pinch-zoom, a second low-quality frame sent ahead to the model to check whether a body part was even in shot, and a final pass that burned the ruler into the saved image. It was 454 lines, it worked, and I was pleased with it. I removed it in the very next commit, hours later, because holding a phone one-handed at an unknown distance over your own forearm does not produce a measurement, and the schema behind it, width and height and area and depth in millimeters, was asking a patient for a precision the physical situation could never give.

How I Worked to Pivot — I went back to the operating system's own photo picker, the camera everybody already knows how to work. The next day I deleted the model behind it too: one migration dropped width, height, area, depth, texture, shape and severity, and put back two soft signals, a color depth and a rough size, compared against the previous photo filed under the same label. The output is not a measurement. It is one of four words, initial, improving, worsening, no change, which answers is it getting better rather than pretending to answer by how much.

What I Traded Away

Some of these were choices and some are simply things I did not finish. I would rather list them than let the screenshots imply otherwise.

A report you can address but not send

01

Profile has a slot for your doctor and one for an emergency contact, the home screen has a Send Report dialog where you tick who should receive it, and the database carries a notify-on-alert flag waiting for it. The send does nothing. It closes the dialog, nothing reads that flag, and there is no provider side of this product at all. I designed the entire escalation surface and shipped none of the escalation, and a button that looks like it works is the piece of this project I am least comfortable with.

Reminders that only live on a calendar

02

The scheduling itself is careful. Doses round up to the next half hour so someone discharged at three in the afternoon still sees tonight's pills, and dragging one dose shifts every later dose of that medication, because people move a whole routine rather than a single pill. But the notification library is installed and configured down to the brand color and no file imports it, and the reminder column is written by nothing. For a product whose premise is that people forget, a calendar you have to remember to open is the widest gap on this page.

The language promise

03

The situation I started from includes families where the paperwork is in English and the patient is not. There is no translation layer in the app. Every string is hardcoded English, the settings row ships a language field nothing reads, speech is pinned to US English, and sign-up accepts a ten-digit US number and nothing else. The pipeline can read a Spanish pill bottle on the way in. The app cannot answer in Spanish.

Designed for low literacy, not yet for a screen reader

04

I built carefully for someone who cannot comfortably read the screen: read-aloud everywhere, hold to talk instead of typing, one sentence per bullet, large touch targets. Then I shipped it with no accessibility labels, roles or hints anywhere, and with every font size written as a fixed number, so Dynamic Type does nothing. The app will read a medication aloud from a button a screen reader cannot name. The one rule I did hold to is that no status in the product depends on color alone; every colored chip carries its word as well.

What Actually Shipped

Designed, built and submitted by one person to The iOS App Store
Owned end to end, home screen through database 20 screens, 11 tables, 30 migrations
From the first commit to the store link Under eight weeks
Written to the database only after A person confirms every record

The honest headline here is scope and shipping, not numbers. One person designed and built twenty screens, eleven Postgres tables across thirty migrations, four serverless functions and an extraction prompt of about a hundred and fifty lines that has to survive a photo of a curved bottle, then carried it through store review and the parts nobody screenshots: an in-app privacy policy, a consent gate that names Google's Gemini and lists exactly which seven kinds of health data leave the phone, and account deletion behind a dialog where you have to type the word DELETE. The consent gate shipped about a month after the build was approved for review rather than before it, which was compliance catching up with me rather than foresight, and I would rather say that plainly.

There is no analytics in this app, no telemetry and no event logging, so I cannot give you a retention number, an extraction accuracy rate, or anything at all about readmissions. Reducing thirty-day readmission is the goal I wrote at the top of the project. It is not a result I measured and I will not present it as one. What I can defend is what the design prevents: because no model output is written before a person confirms it, the worst case is a wrong card you can see and correct, not a wrong dose sitting quietly in your calendar.

Final Takeaways & Lessons Learned

  • The best thing I designed is a step that slows the user down, and it is the only reason I would let this app anywhere near a medication schedule.
  • A familiar tool beats a better one, which is why the system photo picker shipped and my ruler camera lasted a few hours.
  • Precision I cannot stand behind is worse than a coarse answer someone can act on, so four words beat four decimal places.
  • Interface built ahead of capability stops being a placeholder and quietly becomes a promise, which is why Send Report is mine to finish.
  • Designing for a low-literacy reader is not the same as designing for a screen reader, and I assumed the first covered the second.
