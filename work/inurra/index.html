iNurra

Home care after the hospital

View on the App Store

PROJECT OVERVIEW

One photo into a home care plan

Point the camera at a pill bottle, a discharge sheet or a rash. iNurra reads it back in plain words and puts the doses on a calendar.

It is on the App Store, and I did all of it. The research, the screens, the words on them, the store submission. I am not a clinician, so I designed around what a person is holding when they get home.

Sole designer and builder

4 months

Designed and built solo, and AI-paired throughout with Claude Code.

Three in the afternoon

A man leaves the hospital at three in the afternoon, holding a folder. Inside is a page of instructions in small type. Three prescriptions, a follow-up date, and a line about checking his blood pressure daily.

Two of the pills are white. The directions say b.i.d. and t.i.d. Nobody stayed long enough to say that one means twice a day and the other three times.

By Thursday he has missed a dose. On paper that is a patient who did not follow the plan. In his kitchen it is a tired man who cannot tell two white pills apart. The job was never to remind him. It was to translate.

Eight people, their own paperwork

No budget and no access to patients, so I asked family and friends of family. Over two weeks I sat with eight people. Four had come home from hospital that year, four had nursed someone who had. I asked each to read their own paperwork aloud and tell me what it meant.

Three things that changed my mind

The same three things came up again and again. Each one took something off the screens I had already drawn.

01

Confusion, not refusal

Nobody I met had decided to skip a pill. Six of the eight had misread a dose, their own or one they were giving. So I stopped drawing reminders and started writing plain sentences.

02

People know the bottle, not the drug

Two of the eight could name a medicine. The rest reached for the bottle and described the cap. I threw out my neat pill icons. Every medicine now leads with your own photo of it.

03

Nobody can name their own paperwork

I asked people what the page in their hand was called. Four of the eight had no answer. One discharge sheet is a prescription, an appointment and a chore at once.

Three calls that shaped it

Three calls did most of the work. Each one answers a finding above, and each cost me something.

Nothing is saved until you say yes

01

The app reads your photo and suggests. You tap through the cards and fix anything wrong before it writes a word. If the problem is confusion, an app that saves quietly is just faster confusion. It cost me the magic demo, and five bottles now means five reviews.

One camera, one question, nothing else

02

The bottom of every screen holds a camera and a button that says Ask Nurra. Five even choices down there means five decisions before you have done anything. It cost me a whole screen. Almost nobody found the chat behind that button.

The app never asks what you are holding

03

I had built a screen asking what kind of page you were holding. After the fourth blank look I cut it. Now the app works it out, and one sheet can be three things at once. When it guesses wrong, you have to catch that in the review.

01 Photo Point at a bottle or a sheet
02 Describe Add a note, or hold to talk
03 Read The photo comes back in plain words
04 What it found Three medicines, one appointment, two readings
05 Your check You confirm or correct each card
06 Calendar Doses land on tonight and after
07 Listen Any card reads itself aloud

The marked step is the one I would not automate. Everything before it is a suggestion.

Where it broke

I went back to the same eight people, three rounds, at their kitchen tables. Five were over sixty. I handed each one a real pill bottle and said, "put this in the app."

People saved without reading

01

In round one the review was one long scroll. Six of eight tapped save in under five seconds. So I put a plain list of what it found first, then gave every item its own card.

A ruler nobody else ever saw

02

I built a camera with a ruler over the picture, to measure a rash. I shot the same mark twice and got two different sizes. I cut it the same afternoon, and the millimeters became four words: initial, improving, worsening, no change.

The voice read numbers, not meaning

03

Read-aloud spoke a blood pressure as two numbers. Two people heard it and still asked me if that was bad. Now it says higher than normal first, then the numbers.

Where it landed

7 of 8 finished on their own in the last round
31 seconds spent checking before saving, up from 4 in round one
6 of 8 caught a wrong dose I planted, last round

iNurra is on the App Store. I designed it, built it and took it through review alone. There is no tracking in it, so I have no download or retention numbers. What I have is eight people using it in front of me.

Three things are unfinished. The report you address to your doctor does not send, and reminders sit on a calendar you have to open. An app that reads itself aloud still has buttons a screen reader cannot name. The man at the top of this page would know his two white pills now. The one who forgets needs a reminder I have not built.

What I took away

  • The best thing I designed makes people slower, on purpose.
  • Four plain words beat the precise number I could not measure.
  • A button that looks finished is a promise, and I still owe one.
